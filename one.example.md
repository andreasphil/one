# Kermit's Late-Night Reflections on Thirty Years in Show Business

It's past midnight and the theater is finally quiet, which is not a
sentence I get to write very often. Everyone else has gone home, or in
Rizzo's case, gone into the walls, and I'm sitting here in the wings with a
cup of tea that went cold an hour ago, thinking about how strange it is
that any of this ever worked. Thirty years. Thirty years of trying to keep
a theater full of frogs, pigs, bears, and at least one creature I still
can't officially classify running on something resembling a schedule, and
somehow, most nights, the curtain still goes up on time.

I remember when it was just a handful of us, doing sketches in front of
whoever would sit still long enough to watch. Fozzie telling jokes to an
audience of exactly one folding chair. Rowlf playing piano to nobody in
particular, which he insisted was better practice anyway, since nobody
booed the piano. I didn't have a theater then, I had an idea, and a banjo,
and a lot of frogs back home who thought I'd lost my mind moving to the
big city to try to make it as, of all things, an entertainer. 🐸

Somewhere along the way the idea turned into this place, and the handful
of us turned into all of you, and I want to be honest, some nights I still
can't quite believe it. I've watched Gonzo get shot out of a cannon more
times than any insurance company should reasonably allow. I've watched
Miss Piggy karate-chop her way through at least three separate
misunderstandings a week, most of which, if we're being fair, she started.
I've watched Animal nearly destroy a drum kit, several drum kits, an
alarming number of drum kits, and somehow every single time the crowd
loved it more than the songs that were actually rehearsed.

> "Kermit, dear, you think too much. That's always been your problem, and
> also somehow your charm."

That's what Piggy told me once, backstage, after a show that had gone
sideways in about six different directions before intermission even
started. She wasn't wrong. I do think too much. I think about whether the
lighting cue landed, whether Fozzie's new material is going to work or
whether we're going to need the hook, whether the Swedish Chef's kitchen
is currently on fire in a way that's part of the act or not part of the
act. I think about all of it, all the time, and somewhere underneath all
that thinking is a genuine, uncomplicated kind of happiness that I've
never quite figured out how to explain to anyone who hasn't stood where I
stand most nights, just off stage left, watching the lights come up on a
room full of people who came here to laugh.

What I keep coming back to, sitting here with my cold tea, is that none of
this makes any sense on paper. A frog running a theater staffed almost
entirely by chaos. A newsflash team that has never once reported real
news. A pair of hecklers who bought season tickets specifically to insult
the show they never miss. A band whose bus may or may not be
road-legal. And yet every single night, out of all that noise, something
happens that feels a lot like magic, or at least like family, which I've
started to think might be the same thing wearing a different hat.

I don't know how many more years of this I've got in me, or in this
theater, or in whatever this whole strange operation actually is. But I
know that tomorrow the lights will come up again, and Fozzie will tell a
joke that doesn't land, and Gonzo will attempt something that shouldn't be
attempted, and somehow, by the end of the night, it will have been worth
it. It always is. That's the part I never manage to explain properly in
interviews, so I'm writing it down here instead, late, alone, with cold
tea, so that some version of me remembers it on the nights when it feels
like too much.

It's not too much. It's just the show. Wocka wocka, I suppose, even though
that's Fozzie's line and not mine. Time to go home.

#kermit #backstage 🐸
